title: Being honest about what needs work
date: 2026-10-18
summary: Being honest about setbacks, working with your constraints, and finding people whose strengths fit the work.
read: 5 min
---

I think a lot of getting better comes down to being honest about what isn’t working, then doing something with that information.

Say you don’t get the job. Maybe the timing was bad. Maybe somebody else had a connection. Maybe you also struggled to explain your work.

You don’t always get to know exactly what happened. Luck matters, and one rejection tells you very little. But if you keep running into the same problem, it’s worth looking at your own part in it.

You can be disappointed and still ask what you could do differently next time.

## Get specific

“I’m bad at communicating” doesn’t give you much to work with. “I couldn’t explain why I built it that way” gives you something to practice.

If you’re getting interviews but struggling in them, more applications won’t fix that part. Walk someone through your work and ask them to stop you when something isn’t clear. If you aren’t getting interviews, look at the roles you’re targeting and how you’re presenting your experience.

The same goes for feedback at work. Ask for an example. Find out what the other person needed and what was missing. You can decide whether you agree once you understand what they’re saying.

It’s easy to get defensive when you’ve worked hard on something. But explaining away every concern from a coworker means giving up information that could help you. Being confident in your ability should leave some room for someone else to notice something you haven’t.

## Work with the circumstances you have

Some things you’re dealing with are outside your control. They still need to be part of the plan.

If you’re a parent with five hours a week to work toward a career change, a plan that needs twenty hours probably won’t work. You might need a smaller project, more help, or a longer timeline. Be honest about the time you have.

Or say you’re an immigrant facing an employer who discounts your qualifications. That’s unfair. You might put more effort into finding employers familiar with your background, meeting people who can speak to your work, or building something that makes your ability easier to judge. Those choices give you something to act on, even though they can’t guarantee fair treatment.

Sometimes you do need to work harder. Sometimes you need to aim that work somewhere else. If a project is eating up money you can’t spare, making it smaller might help more than putting another month into it.

The response should fit the actual problem.

## Keep your focus

You can put most of your energy into one thing. You don’t have to become average at everything, and you don’t have to personally fix every gap.

Say you’re good at backend debugging, but building a usable frontend takes you forever. Working with someone strong at frontend could make a bigger difference than spending months trying to get as good as them. You can still learn enough to work well together.

The same applies to choosing a cofounder. I’d start by breaking down what the company needs to succeed, what you already bring, and what’s missing.

Take a robotics company that has funding, is known in its field, and has warm introductions to the robotics engineers who might buy its product. Those introductions still need to turn into customers. But if the main thing holding the company back is making the product work reliably, a founder with deep robotics experience might need a cofounder who’s especially strong at software.

Both people are technical. They bring different abilities to the problem that needs solving.

Now take a CRM. You can build the software, you have some sales experience, and the product is fairly general. Your bet is that you can market it better and reach customers the other options aren’t reaching.

In that case, I’d look for someone who’s good at figuring out who those customers are, how to reach them, and why they’d choose your product. That person might also be a great engineer. Marketing is the ability you need them to bring, and the two of you still have to find out whether that proposed advantage holds up.

There’s no fixed rule that one founder should be technical and the other should handle the business. Look at what this particular company needs and whether the team can cover it.

And be realistic about how much help you need. Some gaps call for a cofounder; others could be covered by a hire, a contractor, or a better process. Admitting that someone else could handle part of the work better lets you spend more time where you’re useful.

## Make it possible to be honest at work

The people around you matter for another reason: you need to be able to tell them when something isn’t going well.

“I underestimated this.” “I need help with the client conversations.” “I can’t finish all of this in the time we have.”

A good team can take those statements and work out what to do. Maybe someone joins the next call. Maybe you change the scope or agree on what gets done first.

People still need to own their mistakes and follow through. Managers need to look at unclear expectations or conflicting priorities too. If admitting a problem gets someone embarrassed in front of the team, you’ve made it harder for them to raise the next one.

## Do something with the feedback

This applies outside work too. Sometimes drinking or drug use has started getting in the way of someone’s commitments. Sometimes they’re avoiding difficult conversations or keeping busy with easy tasks so they never have to revisit the thing that went badly.

Be honest about what that pattern is costing you and what would help you change it. That might include asking for support or getting professional help.

For a setback you’re trying to learn from, keep the review simple:

- What happened?
- What do I actually know about why?
- What’s one thing I can change?
- How will I tell whether it helped?

Then make that change. Practice the explanation. Ask someone to join the meeting. Find a collaborator who covers the gap. Cut the project down to something you can finish.

You can stay committed to the goal while changing how you get there. Give yourself room to be wrong about the approach, and use what you learn on the next attempt.
