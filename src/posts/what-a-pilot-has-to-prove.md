---
title: What a Pilot Has to Prove
date: 2026-10-18
summary: A useful pilot solves one real problem, gives its champion evidence and makes the next buying decision possible.
read: 4 min
---

My experience in enterprise and mid-market sales has made me care a lot about what happens after a good demo. The customer likes it. The team is excited. Can anyone explain what has to happen for them to actually use it?

Before a pilot starts, I want to know what decision it is supposed to make possible. Otherwise, another feature can always become the condition for moving forward.

## Finish problem one

Solve problem one before promising problem two. Every extra workflow adds another dependency and another way for the finish line to move.

Take a hypothetical support agent. Start with one ticket category, the systems it needs and the person who will handle exceptions. Get that workflow running with actual users. When someone suggests expanding it, ask whether the new work is necessary to evaluate the original problem. If it isn't, give it a separate decision.

This is why I recommend [three-to-five-hour building sessions](#/blog/getting-into-ai-from-another-major). Can you produce a bounded, runnable slice and explain its limits? Enterprise production adds access reviews, operating responsibilities and dependencies that a short session cannot establish.

I like Paul Graham's [Do Things That Don't Scale](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html) because direct, sometimes manual work with early customers helps you learn what to build. Sit with the user. Fix the integration. Watch the exception. Track how much intervention you supply, though, so the pilot reveals what operation will cost.

## Give your champion something they can use

Your champion has a job outside your pilot. They may be trying to clear a backlog, hit a team target or make the case for a promotion. Supporting you puts some of their reputation behind the result.

Help them make a credible internal case. What improved? For whom? What did it cost, including their team's time? What still needs work? Their manager should be able to follow the evidence without attending your demo.

This is where [forward-deployed engineering](#/blog/the-rise-of-the-forward-deployed-engineer) picks up a lot of sales. A good relationship makes it easier for someone to tell you that security is blocked, the operator hates the workflow or the budget owner has different priorities. That information changes what you should do next.

I'd rather learn those things early than mistake friendly calls for buying intent. The [communication work](#/blog/everything-is-sales) matters most when it helps both sides say something inconvenient.

## Agree on the decision before the build

I want the pilot plan to name:

- The current baseline, target outcome and representative cases used to measure it.
- The customer operator, internal champion and person with buying authority.
- The data access, security review and operating dependencies required for production.
- The decision date, proposed production scope, pricing and approvals still outstanding.

If passing the pilot is supposed to trigger a production purchase, spell out the scope, price and remaining approvals in the agreement. A successful technical evaluation should lead to a defined decision. It shouldn't uncover, for the first time, that nobody has discussed a production budget.

Pricing is part of this. I'm wary of a cheap pilot that avoids the budget conversation production will require. A larger commitment may bring that conversation forward. It can also select for customers who already have budget and urgency. A higher conversion rate wouldn't tell me how much came from the price itself.

Past investment can keep a so-so pilot alive. Some of that can be sunk-cost thinking. Some is the real future cost of replacing the vendor and repeating approvals. I'd account for both when interpreting a conversion. Neither tells me whether the product has earned a renewal.

Paid and unpaid pilots can both make sense. Early product learning might justify an unpaid one. I'd still want customer time, access, a deadline and an explicit next step. Giving away a small evaluation is different from agreeing to an expanding implementation for free.

## The eval decides what you build

For the support agent, measuring response speed alone could reward a system that creates more cleanup. Include the operator's review time, corrections and reopened tickets. Define which actions require approval, what the agent must never do and how an interrupted run resumes without repeating an action.

Choose those cases with the people who understand the workflow. Let AI help implement the checks, then challenge what they leave out. If difficult tickets disappear from the evaluation, the reported improvement may come from changing the workload.

That is the practical side of [the verification problem](#/blog/the-verification-bottleneck). An eval tells the team which changes count as progress. Writing it well means deciding what the customer actually needs, including the costs that an attractive demo can hide.
