I keep noticing how many career paths ask you to persuade someone. You need a customer to buy, a colleague to trust your design, or a team to believe a problem deserves its time. Even when your title has nothing to do with sales, communication changes what you can get done.

An engineer proposing an architecture is asking others to accept its costs and tradeoffs. A manager makes a case for priorities, headcount and time. A founder has to earn the confidence of customers, recruits and investors, often with a different explanation for each.

I see a similar pattern in finance and professional services. Part of a consulting or investment banking partner's work resembles an account executive's: build relationships, understand a client's problem and earn business. An investor earns founders' trust and makes an investment case to colleagues. A CEO does plenty of selling too. These roles have substantial responsibilities beyond sales, but the comparison explains why communication keeps mattering as responsibility grows.

Even a CTO moving into investing would need to turn technical judgment into an argument others understand. Knowing something and explaining why it matters are separate skills. I want to develop both.

## Start close to the work

I still wouldn't make pure sales my default starting point. I'd begin as close to the actual work as possible.

People much more successful than me would recommend starting in sales. I understand the appeal: you learn to ask questions, hear objections and face rejection. Some sales roles require deep technical knowledge. I just think hands-on experience gives those conversations a foundation that is difficult to substitute.

Imagine I'm buying complicated AI software. If I understand its limitations better than the person selling it, I lose confidence. I want to know what happens when the model fails, how the system fits our environment and what the team can support.

Or imagine I'm pitching a company that produces data for reinforcement learning. I'd want an investor to engage with why a lab would use the data and how we could establish its value in post-training. For a drone company, I'd want curiosity about engineering and operating constraints. I wouldn't expect them to know more than every specialist. I would want enough shared understanding to have a useful disagreement.

That connection is especially visible in [forward-deployed engineering](#/blog/the-rise-of-the-forward-deployed-engineer). Understanding the work helps you ask better questions, recognize missing assumptions and make promises people can deliver. A manager can respect a specialist's deeper knowledge while understanding the consequences of a decision.

I think this extends to the trades. If I wanted to build a plumbing or electrical business, I'd want firsthand understanding of the jobs, customer problems and mistakes that make work expensive. That knowledge would inform how I hired, estimated and scaled.

## Keep an outsider's questions

I'm skeptical of a fixed career ladder: spend years as an operator, collect promotions, then earn permission to build something yourself. You can learn, build, sell and lead in parallel. What matters to me is understanding the problem well enough to take responsibility for your decisions.

Experience can make inherited habits feel inevitable. Someone entering from outside may ask why a step exists at all. Perhaps it protects against a real failure. Perhaps it exists because of a tool nobody uses anymore. Those deserve different responses.

A little ignorance can produce a useful question. It doesn't make the answer correct. I'd want to test the unfamiliar approach, learn why the existing one developed and keep whichever constraints survive that examination. Sometimes the experienced person is protecting you from an expensive mistake. Sometimes the conditions have changed.

In a new field, I would want to learn the mechanisms from first principles while building. Understanding the work and questioning its inherited playbook can happen together.

That's one reason I like [people arriving in engineering from different majors](#/blog/getting-into-ai-from-another-major). Economics, philosophy, physics or business can give someone different starting questions. The value comes from bringing those questions into contact with the technical work and learning enough to check them.

I think about it like two coding agents examining the same problem. One proposes an approach; the other notices an assumption it skipped. Different answers create something to investigate. Agreement still needs evidence, and disagreement still needs resolution.

I wouldn't treat my own path as proof of a universal formula. Luck and opportunity matter. I want to understand the work, keep asking an outsider's questions and practice explaining the answer. That's the kind of person I'd want to buy from, work with or trust with a difficult decision.
