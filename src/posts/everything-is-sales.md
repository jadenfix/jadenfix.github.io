An engineer asking for two weeks to fix the backend is making a case for time somebody wanted to spend elsewhere. They need to explain what breaks, why it matters and why this is the right moment to work on it. That looks a lot like sales to me.

A manager makes the same case for priorities and headcount. A founder or CEO has to earn the confidence of customers, recruits and investors, often with a different explanation for each.

In consulting and investment banking, part of a partner's job resembles an account executive's: build relationships, understand a client's problem and earn business. An investor has to earn founders' trust and make an investment case to colleagues. Even a CTO moving into investing needs to turn technical judgment into an argument other people can assess. These roles have other responsibilities, but persuasion keeps showing up.

## Start close to the work

I still wouldn't make pure sales my default starting point. I'd begin as close to the actual work as possible.

People much more successful than me would recommend starting in sales. I understand the appeal: you learn to ask questions, hear objections and face rejection. Some sales roles require deep technical knowledge.

Hands-on work gives you a better sense of what you're promising. You can tell which request is a small configuration change, which one means building a second product, and when you need to say you don't know. That changes how you sell.

Imagine I'm buying complicated AI software. If I understand its limitations better than the person selling it, I lose confidence. I want to know what happens when the model fails, how the system fits our environment and what the team can support.

Or imagine I'm pitching a company that produces data for reinforcement learning. I'd want an investor to engage with why a lab would use the data and how we could establish its value in post-training. For a drone company, I'd want curiosity about engineering and operating constraints. I wouldn't expect them to know more than every specialist. I would want enough shared understanding to have a useful disagreement.

That is part of what attracts me to [forward-deployed engineering](#/blog/the-rise-of-the-forward-deployed-engineer): staying involved when a promise becomes an implementation.

I think this extends to the trades. If I wanted to build a plumbing, electrical or construction business, I'd want firsthand understanding of the jobs, customer problems and mistakes that make work expensive. That knowledge would inform how I hired, estimated and scaled.

## Keep an outsider's questions

I'm skeptical of a fixed career ladder: spend years as an operator, collect promotions, then earn permission to build something yourself. You can learn, build, sell and lead in parallel. What matters to me is understanding the problem well enough to take responsibility for your decisions.

Experience can make inherited habits feel inevitable. Imagine a team saying every output needs manual review. I'd ask which failures that review catches. Maybe it is essential. Maybe the workflow still reflects a model they stopped using six months ago.

Someone coming from outside may ask a question the team stopped asking. A little ignorance can be useful there. It doesn't make the answer correct. I'd test whether the proposed change preserves what the old process protected, then decide what to keep.

That's one reason I like [people arriving in engineering from different majors](#/blog/getting-into-ai-from-another-major). Economics, philosophy, physics or business can give someone different starting questions. The value comes from bringing those questions into contact with the technical work and learning enough to check them.

I think about it like two coding agents examining the same problem. One proposes an approach; the other notices an assumption it skipped. Different answers create something to investigate. Agreement still needs evidence, and disagreement still needs resolution.

I want enough experience doing the work to make better promises, and enough distance from the usual process to ask why we're doing it that way.
